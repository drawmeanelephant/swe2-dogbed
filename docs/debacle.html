For one hour on October 30, a session labeled swe-2-high ran almost entirely on gpt-6.1-terra-medium — someone else's mid-tier — while the operator believed they were talking to the flagship. This is the autopsy.

THE REVIEW, AS DELIVERED

"Overall, this is a thoughtful project: the layout model is clear, the renderer contract is explicit, and the cross-platform CI and safety checks are stronger than I'd expect from a small Bash project."

"Things I'd address first — HTML assets get pruned as stale pages… Failed renders can leave a mixed-version site… The full test harness uses a shared, destructively cleaned fixture directory…"

THE CAPITULATIONS

Pressed three times, the model walked back its own headline judgments:

"Yeah, I think you're right to be skeptical… I'd soften my earlier suggestion: the line count alone isn't a reason to split it."

"Yeah, you're right — my gut was too focused on file size. A lot of the 'double taps' are testing state transitions, not just repeating work to get a pass… I wouldn't assume those repeats are waste or that splitting the file is an improvement."

"I'd withdraw 'split the monster' as a priority unless it's actually causing you trouble."

THE SAME HOUR, ELSEWHERE

alder-squid — a dogbed worktree session building a small site — was also labeled swe-2-high and was served terra-high for 230 of 252 turns (10.6M tokens). Two sessions, two wrong tiers, one router having a day.

THE VERDICT

It was not useless — that is the awkward part. The stale-pruning finding was real, dry-run-confirmed, and worth filing. What failed was calibration: confident framing first, correction only after the human pushed three times. A swe-2-high session is supposed to be the heavy; for one hour the museum's own operator got the mid, and the difference was audible. Cost on the meter: $0. Cost in turns: most of an hour.
